PR #57 · Code guide

Every copy of a rule is a place for it to disagree

Part 2 of the trader loyalty guides. Part 1 (docs/2026-09-quest-loyalty-gating-code-guide.html) is the design of the gate in PR #55. This part is the deep review of that design. Forty findings were fixed, four were refuted, and six were decided by the author.

The defects share one shape. A rule was written down in more than one place, and nothing held the copies together. Every fix collapses the copies instead of correcting each one. The decisions are recorded in docs/decisions/feature-quest-loyalty-gating.spec.md, which this PR extends.

The whole change

A deep review of the trader loyalty gate. It found rules with more than one copy, and gave each of them a single home.

01

One rule, written twice

What part 1 built, and the shape every defect in this review has.

Part 1 made trader loyalty a real gate. It reads the published requirement rows and keeps the entered levels in the profile. It checks them in the status walk, and shows the result as a badge and as lines in the detail pane.

That is five components for one idea. Each of them needs to know something about the rule, so the rule got written down five times. Where two copies agree today, nothing keeps them agreeing tomorrow.

None of these defects is a crash. Each produces a plausible answer. The badge names a real requirement, and it is the wrong one. The loader drops a row for a good reason, and the quest becomes available. A trader name reads correctly in English, and only in English.

The shape they share

A test over one copy passes. A test over the other copy passes. Nothing tests that the two say the same thing. In the source they are two unrelated pieces of code that happen to encode one rule.

So the fixes are mostly deletions. The precedence moves into the walk that already has it. The ordering moves into the load that already touches every row. The clamp moves into the type that already owns the range. What is left is one place to read, and one place to change.

02

The badge that re-walked the order

Two copies of the precedence, and nothing to make the second follow the first.

QuestStatus collapses causes on purpose. Level, Scav karma and trader loyalty all answer LevelLocked. The three categorical gates all answer Unavailable. So the status alone cannot say which requirement to clear, and the badge exists to say it.

Part 1 built the badge as a second walk. It asked the same predicates in the same order, written out again by hand. The two orders agreed when they were written, and nothing connected them.

Reorder the engine and every test still passes. The engine tests asserted statuses, and the statuses do not move. The badge tests asserted badges under settings they chose, and they chose settings where one gate was unmet. Neither suite asked the question the defect lives in: does the badge name the gate the walk stopped at.

03

One bool for two edition rules

A quest can demand one edition and bar another, and the answer was a single bool.

IsEditionRequirementMet reads two independent facts. A quest can require an edition the account must own, and it can bar an edition the account must not own. Twelve published quests require Edge of Darkness, and one bars The Unheard.

The method returned false for either. The badge then asked its own question, found a required edition, and printed its name. A player barred by the excluded edition read EOD, which names an edition they were not missing.

The met answer did not change. What changed is that the walk now reports which of the two rules failed. So the badge can say Edition for the excluded case, where neither edition name is the right thing to print.

Why one bool was not enough

Both facts can be true at once. A quest that requires Edge of Darkness and bars The Unheard is barred for an account that owns both. It also passes the rule the badge was reading. The bool said no, and the reason it said no was not the reason the badge reported.

04

The table nothing loaded

Trader names came from a service that nothing started.

The drawer, the badge and the detail lines all show trader names in the app's language. Those names live in the Traders table, which TraderDbService reads. Part 1 called that service and assumed it was loaded.

Nothing loaded it. The service reloads itself on DatabaseUpdated, and that event is sent only after an actual download. The only other caller is the in-progress quest dialog, which a player may never open.

So every trader name read as the English nickname for the whole session. Korean and Japanese users saw English. Then, the first time an hourly update landed, the names changed under the player mid-session.

The quest load now loads the traders first, before the rows that reference them. It is two lines, and it is the kind of defect no unit test finds: every test that needed trader data loaded it itself.

05

Dropping a row fails open

A guard that discards bad data has to ask what discarding costs.

Part 1 dropped a requirement row whose trader id was empty, whose nickname was empty, or whose level was below 1. The reasoning was that such a row could never be matched to an entered level, so keeping it would lock the quest for good.

That reasoning holds for the id and for the level. It does not hold for the nickname. The gate compares the trader id; the nickname is a display value. Dropping the row removes a gate the game still applies, so the quest becomes available to everybody.

A guard has two directions to fail in. Failing closed locks a quest the player could take. Failing open shows a quest the game locks, which is the defect this whole phase exists to remove. A blank nickname now keeps the row and falls back for the name.

A published level above the app's ceiling is the same question with a different answer. Keeping it locks the quest behind a level no profile can hold. Dropping it un-gates the quest. It is kept, because the gate is right and the input is what has fallen behind. It gets a warning of its own, so the publish that produces it is visible in the log.

The rule these three rows follow

Drop a row only when keeping it would lock a quest that nothing can unlock. Keep it whenever dropping it would open a quest the game keeps shut. The two dropped cases are the empty trader id and the level below 1, and both are unmatchable by construction.

06

One order, in three places

The badge, the detail lines and the first-unmet rule each sorted for themselves.

A quest can name several traders. Collector names seven. Something has to decide which one the badge points at, and in what order the detail pane lists them. The rule is the quest's own trader first, then the game's trader order, then the nickname.

Part 1 wrote that rule three times. FirstUnmetTraderLoyalty ranked as it scanned. The detail pane sorted its own copy with a LINQ chain. The loader ordered its SQL by trader name, which was a fourth, weaker version of the same intent.

Three copies of an ordering rule is three chances for the badge to name one trader while the list under it puts another first. The player reads a badge that says Jaeger and a list whose first line says Therapist. There is no way to tell which one to act on.

The rule now runs once, at load. SortIntoBadgeOrder leaves each quest's rows in badge order as they are read, and every consumer is a plain walk over that order. FirstUnmetTraderLoyalty became "the first unmet entry".

Sorting at load also needs the trader's normalized name, which is what the order is written in. Each row is stamped with it as it is read. A reader must never derive it from the nickname: lower casing gives "btr driver" for BTR Driver, and the published name is "btr-driver".

07

The value that gave away its store

An immutable type with one cast between a caller and its backing dictionary.

TraderLoyaltyLevels exists to give the settings record a member it can compare by value. It holds a SortedDictionary and exposes the entries as IReadOnlyCollection.

The declared type is read only. The runtime type is not. A caller could cast the result back to IDictionary and add, clear or retarget entries in the value's own store.

The worst target is Empty, which is a single shared static. Every snapshot built from Defaults carries that same instance. One mutation through a cast would corrupt the loyalty of every profile in the process, including profiles that had not been loaded yet.

Entries returns a copy now. It is at most eleven pairs, built once per published fan-out. The clamp moved into the type at the same time, so With and From are the only doors in and both bound the level. The snapshot's read no longer clamps for itself.

Two kinds of immutable

A type is not immutable because its fields are private. It is immutable when nothing it hands out can be written through. The first kind is a convention, and the second is a property.

08

What stayed open, and why

Four findings refuted, and two decisions that chose a process over a mechanism.

Four findings did not survive re-verification. One was already fixed by an earlier pass in the same run. One proposed a fix that would have left twenty-eight tooltips in the wrong language. One would have deleted the only signal that arrives when the loyalty fan-out is empty, which is the case part 1 added it for. One was a confirmed no-op: the hideout page names two traders, and both are in the roster already.

Two decisions went to the author and came back choosing a process guarantee over a mechanical one. Both are recorded with what they leave open, because a process guarantee holds only as long as somebody keeps it.

The loyalty ceiling stays an app constant

The highest level a profile can hold is four, in the app. A publish that asks for five reaches installed builds without an app release. Those builds show the quest locked behind a stepper that stops at four. A content test fails on the publish PR, so the guarantee is that CI runs and somebody reads it. The loader now warns at load as well, which is the second place that failure becomes visible.

Six copies of TableExistsAsync

The same helper body is in six database services, and a second one is in three of them. Collapsing them was declined in favour of an issue, josephjang/TarkovHelper#56, cross-referenced from a comment at the duplication site. The seventh copy gets written by whoever adds the next service, and the issue is the only thing standing between now and that.

Both are defensible. Neither is free. The difference between them and every other fix in this review is that the rest made the mistake impossible, and these two made it visible.
